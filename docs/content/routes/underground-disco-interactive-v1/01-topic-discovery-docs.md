# Blank-Slate Narrative Topic Discovery

## Goal

Generate fresh narrative topic ideas for a place-based music history project without inheriting assumptions, themes, structures, or candidate events from earlier work.

The output should be a set of **research-derived topic candidates**, not finished narratives.

## Process

### 1. Reset the narrative frame

Treat the subject area as unexplored.

Do not begin from:

* previous narrative structures
* previously selected genres or communities
* existing event candidates
* earlier map concepts
* assumptions about which stories are most important

Existing project knowledge may help interpret findings, but it should not determine what is searched for.

### 2. Scan broadly across music history

Search across different:

* eras
* neighborhoods
* communities
* genres
* institutions
* forms of musical activity

The aim is breadth before depth.

For example, searches might uncover:

* migration and immigrant music
* nightlife
* publishing
* recording
* performance spaces
* informal music-making
* clubs and dance culture
* radio
* music businesses
* neighborhood scenes
* artistic or technological changes

### 3. Prefer strong historical sources

Use credible sources that can support later research, such as:

* libraries and archives
* museums
* universities
* historical societies
* government preservation records
* major cultural institutions
* primary-source collections

At this stage, the goal is not exhaustive sourcing. It is to determine whether a potential topic has enough documentary support to justify further research.

### 4. Look for narrative tensions, not just subjects

Do not turn every genre or scene into a topic.

Instead ask what historical question the material suggests.

For example:

* How did an immigrant neighborhood influence mainstream culture?
* Why did a particular musical scene cluster in one place?
* How did music move between communities?
* How did technology change nightlife?
* Why did a music scene relocate?
* How did private and commercial musical spaces differ?

A promising topic should contain some kind of **change, conflict, movement, transformation, or relationship**.

### 5. Test whether geography matters

For a map-based narrative, location should do more than provide pins.

Ask:

* Does movement between places help explain the story?
* Do neighborhoods represent different communities or functions?
* Did a scene cluster spatially?
* Did music move through venues, streets, businesses, or institutions?
* Did the geography change over time?
* Can spatial relationships reveal something that prose alone would not?

Topics where geography is incidental should be ranked lower.

### 6. Form provisional topic candidates

For each promising thread, write a compact candidate containing:

**Working title**

A memorable description of the historical subject.

**Approximate timeframe**

Enough to define the research boundary.

**Narrative question**

The historical problem or transformation the route could investigate.

**Initial historical rationale**

A short explanation of why the topic appears significant.

**Map potential**

How geography might help communicate the argument.

This is still exploratory. None of these elements should be treated as established narrative conclusions.

### 7. Maintain diversity between candidates

Avoid returning several versions of essentially the same story.

Prefer candidates that differ in:

* historical period
* community
* type of musical activity
* geographic structure
* narrative mechanism

This makes the discovery phase genuinely useful rather than simply generating variations around the first promising idea.

### 8. Compare candidates

Evaluate candidates using criteria such as:

* strength of available evidence
* narrative distinctiveness
* geographic clarity
* availability of identifiable places or events
* potential for visual storytelling
* manageable scope
* difference from existing SoundAtlas routes or research

A candidate can be historically interesting but still be a poor map narrative.

### 9. Identify promising candidates without committing

Select a small number for further investigation.

Explain why they appear promising, but keep the conclusions provisional.

For example:

> This candidate appears strong because the historical evidence, geographic movement, and identifiable places could support a coherent route.

Avoid saying:

> This is the narrative.

The discovery stage should preserve the ability to reject or substantially change the topic after deeper research.

## Output of this stage

The result should be approximately **5–10 narrative topic candidates**, each supported by enough initial research to justify its existence.

The output is **not yet**:

* a chapter structure
* a route
* an event list
* a final thesis
* a complete research dossier

## Recommended next step

Take the strongest 2–3 candidates through a **lightweight evidence and map-potential test**.

For each candidate:

1. identify roughly 5–8 plausible historical events, places, or transitions;
2. verify that reliable sources exist for them;
3. test whether they form a meaningful geographic and chronological structure;
4. check whether the proposed narrative question survives contact with the evidence;
5. reject, reshape, or advance the candidate.

Only after this test should the project begin constructing the actual narrative.
