# Targeted Gap Research

## Goal

Resolve specific weaknesses revealed by a light evidence + route-shape test before locking the route cohort or thesis.

This stage should not restart broad discovery.

Its purpose is to investigate only the gaps that could materially change:

* the thesis
* the candidate cohort
* the geographic structure
* the communities represented
* the route’s historical mechanism

## Inputs

A completed light evidence + route-shape test that has identified:

* a provisional thesis
* a provisional candidate cohort
* initial include/exclude calls
* map relationships
* specific evidence or representation gaps

## Core Principle

Research the weakness, not the whole topic again.

The question is:

> What important part of the story might the current route be missing or misrepresenting?

The goal is to challenge the current route, not simply add more stops.

## Process

### 1. Convert weaknesses into focused research questions

Translate each identified gap into a narrow research task.

For example:

* Is one community underrepresented because the current sources favor another?
* Are there important venues outside the current geographic cluster?
* Is a supposed transition actually supported by evidence?
* Is an important process happening through infrastructure rather than venues?
* Are people, audiences, dancers, businesses, or institutions missing from a venue-centered route?

Avoid generic searches such as:

> history of this genre in the city

Prefer questions such as:

> Which venues serving this underrepresented community operated during the relevant period?

or:

> What infrastructure connected DJs, venues, and record companies?

### 2. Search for alternative historical mechanisms

Do not assume the missing evidence will look like the existing route.

If the current cohort consists mostly of clubs, the gap may be found in:

* community centers
* private homes
* record stores
* record pools
* radio
* promoters
* labels
* dance practices
* social organizations
* neighborhood networks
* informal gatherings

This prevents the structure of the existing route from determining what counts as evidence.

### 3. Prefer evidence that can challenge the cohort

Prioritize findings that could:

* replace an existing candidate
* expose an overrepresented perspective
* introduce a missing community
* reveal a different origin or trajectory
* change the geographic pattern
* alter the route’s chronology
* suggest a different narrative mechanism

A finding that merely confirms an already strong stop is lower priority.

### 4. Distinguish participation from historical agency

Do not treat the presence of a community as sufficient representation.

Ask whether the evidence shows people acting as:

* organizers
* promoters
* owners
* performers
* DJs
* dancers
* producers
* distributors
* innovators
* audiences shaping practice

This may reveal that a community contributed through a different mechanism than the current route assumes.

For example, one group may be represented through venue ownership while another is better understood through dance innovation or record distribution.

### 5. Search for networks, not only landmarks

Look for relationships between places and actors.

Examples:

* one promoter operating several venues
* DJs moving between clubs
* records circulating through a distribution network
* dancers carrying practices between neighborhoods
* one venue influencing another
* businesses linking local scenes to the music industry

Networks often provide stronger route structure than isolated famous places.

### 6. Map new candidates immediately

Add promising findings to the provisional map.

Then compare them with the existing cohort.

Ask:

* Does the geographic center shift?
* Does a new borough become important?
* Do previously separate clusters become connected?
* Does a movement pattern emerge?
* Does the map expose parallel scenes rather than a single lineage?

Do not wait until the end of research to inspect spatial consequences.

### 7. Compare new and existing candidates

For every strong new candidate, ask:

> Does this add something genuinely missing?

and:

> Should it replace an existing candidate rather than simply expanding the route?

Useful outcomes include:

* retain existing candidate
* replace existing candidate
* downgrade to context
* add as core
* add as connector
* keep for later research
* remove from minimum route

The purpose is to improve the cohort, not maximize its size.

### 8. Re-examine the narrative mechanism

New findings may suggest that the original thesis is too linear or too narrow.

For example, an initial route may imply:

> one scene created an innovation that later spread outward

Gap research may instead reveal:

> several communities were developing related practices in parallel, connected by shared infrastructure

Possible structural changes include:

**Linear → branching**

**Single origin → multiple origins**

**Venue history → ecosystem history**

**Genre history → community/infrastructure history**

**Center/periphery → network**

Allow the evidence to change the shape of the story.

### 9. Revise the provisional thesis

Rewrite the thesis only as far as the new evidence justifies.

The revised thesis should account for:

* the original strong evidence
* the newly discovered evidence
* newly visible communities
* new geographic relationships
* any competing historical pathways

Avoid making the thesis broader than the evidence.

### 10. Record unresolved gaps

Targeted gap research can reveal additional uncertainty.

Distinguish between:

**Route-blocking gaps**

These must be resolved before the cohort can be locked.

Examples:

* central community still unsupported
* key transition lacks evidence
* candidate identity is uncertain
* thesis depends on a single retrospective source

**Non-blocking gaps**

These can be handled during later deep research.

Examples:

* exact opening month
* minor address uncertainty
* secondary visual material
* additional corroboration for an already well-supported claim

This prevents endless research.

## Output

A targeted gap research result should contain:

* the gaps investigated
* focused research questions
* new candidate findings
* evidence supporting each new candidate
* geographic implications
* comparison with the previous cohort
* revised candidate statuses
* any candidates replaced or removed
* changes to the route shape
* revised thesis if necessary
* remaining route-blocking gaps
* a decision on whether the cohort is ready to be tested for minimum sufficiency

## Suggested Comparison Format

| Candidate            | Previous status | New evidence                      | Revised status   | Reason                      |
| -------------------- | --------------- | --------------------------------- | ---------------- | --------------------------- |
| Existing candidate A | Core            | Still strongest evidence          | Retain           | Necessary narrative role    |
| Existing candidate B | Include         | Redundant after new finding       | Remove           | Better candidate now exists |
| New candidate C      | —               | Fills missing community/mechanism | Core             | Materially changes route    |
| New candidate D      | —               | Interesting but weakly sourced    | Research further | Not yet defensible          |

## Stop Condition

Targeted gap research is complete when:

1. the specific weaknesses identified by the previous test have been investigated;
2. strong new candidates have either been incorporated or rejected;
3. the thesis has been revised where necessary;
4. remaining uncertainties are explicit;
5. no unresolved gap is likely to fundamentally change the route without being identified.

The next step is then a **minimum-sufficient cohort test**.

That test asks:

> Can the smallest defensible set of retained candidates now explain the narrative from beginning to end?

## Principle

The light evidence + route-shape test asks:

> What seems to be missing?

Targeted gap research asks:

> What happens to the route when we investigate exactly what is missing?

Its value comes from allowing the route to become smaller, broader, narrower, or structurally different based on evidence.
