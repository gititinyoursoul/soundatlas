# Deep Route Research

## Goal

Research each provisionally locked route stop in enough depth to support a defensible final narrative.

At this stage, broad candidate discovery has stopped.

The purpose is to establish:

* what exactly happened
* when and where it happened
* who was involved
* what the evidence supports
* what remains uncertain
* what narrative role the event can legitimately perform

The output is a **research dossier**, not polished route prose.

## Inputs

A provisionally locked minimum-sufficient cohort containing:

* a working thesis
* retained route candidates
* narrative role for each candidate
* known uncertainties
* previous source material from discovery and gap research

## Core Principle

Deep research should strengthen or weaken the retained events based on evidence.

It should not assume that because an event survived earlier testing, every claim previously attached to it is correct.

The question is:

> **What can this event actually demonstrate once researched closely?**

## Process

### 1. Research the retained cohort only

Stop general topic discovery.

Research each retained candidate individually.

New candidates should only be introduced if deep research reveals that an existing route event is untenable or that a major route-breaking gap remains.

This keeps the research bounded.

### 2. Tighten the event boundary

Replace broad labels such as:

> This club was important during the 1970s.

with a defensible event boundary.

Possible boundaries include:

* a first party
* an opening
* a relocation
* a specific period of operation
* the introduction of a new practice
* the creation of an institution
* a documented series of events
* a transition between two forms of activity

Record:

* start date
* end date if relevant
* date precision
* uncertainty

A narrow event is generally easier to support than an entire venue history.

### 3. Verify the location

Establish the exact or best-defensible location.

Record:

* venue or place name
* street address
* neighborhood
* borough or city
* whether the address changed over time
* whether the route event refers to a specific incarnation of the venue

This is especially important when:

* a venue moved
* several venues shared a name
* the relevant event occurred at only one location
* later addresses are more famous than the historically relevant one

### 4. Identify the key actors

Determine who actually performed the action represented by the event.

Actors may include:

* DJs
* musicians
* dancers
* promoters
* owners
* organizers
* audiences
* engineers
* designers
* record companies
* community organizations

Avoid automatically treating the most famous person associated with the place as the event's primary actor.

### 5. Establish what happened

Write a concise factual description of the event based on the strongest available evidence.

Separate:

**Observed/documented facts**

from

**historical interpretation**

For example:

Documented:

> A recurring party began at this location in a particular year.

Interpretation:

> This represented a new model of nightlife.

Both may be useful, but they should not be confused.

### 6. Identify what the event can demonstrate

For every retained event, explicitly define the strongest route claim it can support.

Examples:

* emergence of a new social practice
* development of a DJ technique
* creation of music-distribution infrastructure
* community ownership
* dancer-led innovation
* transformation of venue design
* crossover into commercial culture

This should be more precise than a generic significance statement.

### 7. Identify what the event cannot demonstrate

Record claims that are:

* unsupported
* too broad
* based on weak evidence
* disputed
* reliant on hindsight
* better treated as interpretation

This is particularly important for claims involving:

* “first”
* “invented”
* “originated”
* “the birthplace of”
* “single-handedly changed”
* direct influence between historical actors

Avoid publishing such claims unless the evidence justifies them.

### 8. Upgrade source quality

Move beyond discovery-level sources where possible.

Look for a useful combination of:

**Primary or contemporary sources**

* flyers
* invitations
* advertisements
* newspaper reports
* trade publications
* photographs
* membership cards
* business records
* oral histories
* contemporary interviews

**Strong secondary sources**

* archives
* museums
* historical societies
* scholarly research
* reputable institutional histories

Retrospective testimony can be valuable but should be corroborated when it supports major factual or superlative claims.

### 9. Track source strength by claim

Do not treat an entire source as simply “good” or “bad.”

Ask which claim it supports.

For example:

| Claim                    | Evidence strength |
| ------------------------ | ----------------- |
| Venue existed            | High              |
| Address                  | High              |
| Opening date             | Medium            |
| Ownership                | Medium            |
| “First of its kind”      | Low               |
| Influence on later scene | Medium            |

This makes later narrative review much easier.

### 10. Record uncertainty explicitly

Uncertainty is part of the research output.

Examples:

* conflicting opening dates
* unclear ownership
* approximate event date
* disputed origin claim
* address ambiguity
* retrospective testimony without contemporary corroboration

Classify uncertainty according to whether it is:

**Route-blocking**

Could change whether the event belongs in the route.

**Claim-blocking**

The event can stay, but a particular claim should not be used.

**Minor**

Can be resolved during editing or left appropriately approximate.

### 11. Search for archival and visual material

Identify material that could later support map or narrative presentation.

Examples:

* period photographs
* flyers
* invitations
* posters
* advertisements
* floor plans
* venue exteriors
* dance-floor photographs
* record sleeves
* newspaper clippings
* membership cards

At this stage, note the material and source rather than designing the final visual treatment.

### 12. Re-evaluate the event boundary after research

Deep research often changes what the stop should actually be.

For example:

Initial candidate:

> Venue X

Deep-research event:

> Venue X reopens at a new location and introduces a particular practice, 1974–1977.

Or:

Initial candidate:

> Dance Y begins here.

Deep-research event:

> This community center becomes an important incubator for an already developing dance practice.

This refinement is a successful outcome.

### 13. Compare event types across the cohort

The route should not assume all events must have the same form.

A strong route may contain:

* a party
* a venue opening
* a community gathering
* an organizational formation
* a technological development
* a dance practice
* a distribution network
* a period of experimentation

Varied event types can better represent a cultural ecosystem than a list of venues.

### 14. Re-test candidate viability

After researching each event, assign a confidence level.

For example:

**High confidence**

* event boundary is clear
* multiple sources support major claims
* location is defensible
* route role remains strong

**Medium confidence**

* event is real and relevant
* one or more important claims still need corroboration

**Low confidence**

* event boundary or significance remains weak
* route role depends on unsupported claims

Candidates with low confidence may need replacement or removal.

### 15. Identify research exceptions

If most of the cohort is strong but one event remains weaker, do not restart broad research.

Create a narrow follow-up task.

For example:

> Find contemporary evidence confirming the ownership and opening date of Candidate X.

This preserves momentum while maintaining evidence quality.

## Suggested Event Dossier

Each route event should contain:

### Identity

* Working event title
* Date or date range
* Location
* Actors

### Event boundary

* What begins
* What ends
* Why this timeframe is used

### Evidence summary

* What happened
* Strongest sources

### Route function

* What this event uniquely demonstrates

### Supported claims

* Claims safe to use in narrative

### Unsupported or risky claims

* Claims that need corroboration or should be avoided

### Geographic significance

* Why this location matters to the route

### Archival / visual material

* Available images, flyers, documents, or other artifacts

### Confidence

* High / medium / low

### Remaining research needs

* Specific unresolved questions

| ------- | --------------- | --------------------------- | ----------------- | ---------- | ----------- |

## Output

A completed deep-research stage should produce:

* a research dossier for every retained stop
* precise event boundaries
* verified or best-defensible locations
* identified historical actors
* supported narrative claims
* risky or unsupported claims
* source lists
* source-strength assessment
* archival and visual possibilities
* confidence level for each event
* remaining event-specific research tasks
* indication of whether any candidate should be replaced or removed

## Stop Condition

Deep route research is complete when:

1. every retained event has a defensible historical boundary;
2. the location and key actors are sufficiently established;
3. every event has at least one clear narrative claim supported by evidence;
4. major uncertainties are explicit;
5. no core event depends on an unsupported superlative or origin claim;
6. weak candidates have either been strengthened, narrowed, replaced, or flagged for a focused follow-up;
7. the cohort is stable enough for cross-stop comparison.

The next stage is **cross-stop synthesis**.

## Principle

The minimum-sufficient cohort test asks:

> **Which events are necessary?**

Deep route research asks:

> **What can each of those events actually prove?**

Cross-stop synthesis then asks:

> **What can these events prove together, and how should they connect into one coherent route?**
