The targeted pass **does reshape the disco route again**. The earlier cohort made the history look too much like a genealogy of Manhattan gay clubs. The new evidence shows at least three other mechanisms that deserve to be inside the story: a Black-owned/promoted disco economy, Puerto Rican/Latinx dance-floor innovation, and infrastructure connecting DJs to records and labels. ([sova.si.edu][1])

This map shows the strongest **currently mappable additions/anchors**, not a final route:

### What the gap research found

1. **There was a distinct Black disco-club network that our first test almost completely missed.** The Smithsonian's recently processed *New York City Disco Clubs Collection* covers 1971–82, with the bulk from 1972–75, and says its flyers, cards and other ephemera are almost exclusively for venues catering to Black audiences. That is strong archival evidence that Black disco nightlife was not a marginal add-on to the better-documented gay-club story. ([sova.si.edu][1])

   A particularly useful thread is **The Best of Friends (TBOF)**. Noel Hankin and seven partners initially promoted nights at Ginza and La Martinique for a predominantly Black, white-collar Midtown crowd. They then moved into ownership: Lucifer's in Queens in 1973, Leviticus in Manhattan in 1974, Justine's in 1975, Brandy's in Brooklyn in 1976, and later Bogard's. This gives us something the original cohort lacked: **Black ownership, entrepreneurship and a multi-borough club network**, not just Black attendance at mixed venues. ([Medgar Evers College -][2])

   Ginza is particularly useful for an early candidate because WNET/THIRTEEN identifies the venue at **40 East 58th Street** and documents TBOF's weekly dance party there. ([thirteen.org][3])

2. **The Latino gap is not best solved by simply finding “the Latino disco club.”** The evidence points to a much more distributed contribution. Dance historian Tim Lawrence identifies Latinx dancers as part of mixed private-party culture and documents DJs including David Rodriguez, Richie Rivera and Eddie Rivera. Richie Rivera brought pronounced Latin percussion into his Flamingo sets; venues including the Copacabana, Cork & Bottle and Ipanema catered to Latinx crowds and mixed Latin records with disco. ([timlawrence.info][4])

   There is also a compelling **dancer-led Bronx story**. The Bronx County Historical Society traces Latin Hustle to Puerto Rican teenagers in the South Bronx around 1972 and identifies St. Mary's Recreation Center as an important incubator from summer 1974. From there the dance moved into clubs and larger Manhattan dance spaces. St. Mary's is at 450 Saint Ann's Avenue. ([The Bronx County Historical Society][5])

   This matters conceptually. Our earlier framing concentrated on what **DJs and club designers did to dancers**. Latin Hustle introduces the reverse relationship: **dancers themselves were inventing new ways of using recorded dance music and then carrying those practices into clubs**. Geography becomes useful because this development begins in South Bronx house-party/community-center culture and travels into the wider disco city. ([The Bronx County Historical Society][5])

3. **The missing bridge may be infrastructure rather than another nightclub.** In 1975, at the Loft's new location at **99 Prince Street**, David Mancuso, Vince Aletti and Steve D'Acquisto launched the New York Record Pool. It provided promotional records to DJs and became a meeting/distribution point between DJs and the record business. ([The Village Voice][6])

   This becomes even more significant in light of the Latino research: Eddie Rivera, secretary of the New York Record Pool, created a separate **Latin Music Department** before founding a breakaway organization, the International Disco Record Center. So the record pool can potentially connect several parts of the narrative—clubs, DJs, labels, records and Latino disco networks—in one place. ([timlawrence.info][4])

### What this does to the route

I would now change the provisional cohort approximately like this:

Paradise Garage remains especially useful because it reconnects several strands: from 1977 to 1987 it served a patronage composed substantially of sexual and ethnic minorities, particularly African American gay men, at 84 King Street. ([NYC LGBTQ Historic Sites][7])

### More important: the thesis changes

I would no longer use:

> *Underground queer clubs invented the modern dance space and disco spread outward from there.*

That's becoming too linear and too narrow.

The evidence currently supports something closer to:

> **New York disco emerged through several overlapping experiments in what a dance floor could be. Private queer parties reorganized nightlife around recorded sound and community; Black promoters built new clubs and audiences across the city; Puerto Rican dancers and Latinx DJs changed how the music was danced to and heard; and new record networks connected those dance floors to the music industry.**

That is still provisional, but importantly it arose **because of the gap research**, rather than being decided beforehand. ([sova.si.edu][1])

It also suggests a more interesting route shape than our previous one. Instead of:

**Loft → Gallery → Studio 54 → Paradise Garage**

we're beginning to see **parallel streams that converge and interact**:

**private/queer party culture**
↘

**Black club entrepreneurship** → **shared NYC disco ecosystem** → **Paradise Garage / later dance culture**

**Puerto Rican dance culture**
↗

with the **record pool and DJs acting as connectors between those worlds**.

### Remaining gaps before locking the cohort

I would **not yet call the targeted gap research finished enough to lock the route**. Two narrow issues remain.

The Black-network evidence is compelling, but much of the TBOF story currently rests on Hankin's retrospective account. The Smithsonian archive gives us a promising independent body of period ephemera, including material for Leviticus and many other Black-oriented clubs, but we should inspect those artifacts before deciding which Black-owned venue is the strongest actual route event. ([sova.si.edu][1])

And for the Bronx/Latinx strand, St. Mary's is a very promising candidate, but its exact relationship to **disco history rather than broader New York social-dance history** needs a little more evidence. The Bronx Historical Society clearly establishes Latin Hustle's South Bronx development and spread into the disco-era club scene; the next question is how directly we can tie that movement to the route's central musical argument. ([The Bronx County Historical Society][5])

**Verdict: advance, reshape again.** The gap pass has made the topic stronger. More importantly, it has shifted the route away from “important disco clubs” toward a potentially much richer story about **different communities constructing the New York dance floor from different directions**.
