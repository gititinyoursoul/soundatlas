# Research dossier: New York Builds the Dance Floor

## Research result

The experimental `underground-disco-interactive` artifacts contain enough information to produce a bounded first route. No missing fact blocks Editorial Review or publication, provided the route avoids an exact Leviticus opening-day claim and does not turn parallel developments into a causal chain.

The first publication can use the current SoundAtlas map, timeline, and StoryPanel. Progressive map reveals, graphics, images, media, and explicit event connections remain outside this slice.

## Historical frame

The route asks how a dance floor became a coordinated relationship among records, sound systems, DJs, dancers, social space, nightlife ownership, and record distribution. It does not attempt a comprehensive history of disco or claim that its six examples represent every community in 1970s New York.

Four editorial movements organize the story without requiring new UI behavior:

1. One room: the Loft demonstrates the possibilities of a private, invitation-based musical environment.
2. Parallel agency in 1974: Leviticus, St. Mary's Recreation Center, and the Gallery show promoters, dancers, and DJs changing nightlife in different ways.
3. Infrastructure: the New York Record Pool connects dance floors with record promotion and feedback.
4. Designed synthesis: Paradise Garage shows how extensively a club could be organized around music, sound, dancers, and community by 1977–1978.

## People and forms of agency

- David Mancuso: host and sound-focused organizer at the Loft.
- Best of Friends and Black club promoters: audience-building, ownership, and nightlife organization around Leviticus.
- Puerto Rican South Bronx dancers, including dancers associated with the St. Mary's gatherings: development and circulation of Latin Hustle.
- Nicky Siano and sound-system collaborators: DJ performance, lighting, sound, and room design at the Gallery.
- Paul Casella, Steve D'Acquisto, David Mancuso, participating DJs, and record labels: record-pool organization and feedback.
- Michael Brody, Larry Levan, Richard Long, and the Garage's dance community: venue, programming, sound design, and collective use.

Names support the explanation but do not convert collaborative histories into sole-inventor stories.
