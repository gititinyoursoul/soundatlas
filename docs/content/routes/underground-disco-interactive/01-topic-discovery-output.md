I treated this as a **blank-slate research pass** and deliberately excluded the hip-hop narrative and concepts we had already developed. I searched across Library of Congress, NYPL, Museum of the City of New York, Smithsonian, Carnegie Hall, New-York Historical, and NYC historical records, looking especially for stories where **geography actually explains something about the music**.

The most promising topics I found are:

1. **Songs of Arrival — Yiddish Broadway and the Lower East Side, ca. 1880s–1920s**
   **Narrative question:** *What happens when the music of an immigrant community becomes big-city popular culture?* Yiddish theater flourished around the Bowery and Lower East Side, producing songs, stars, theaters, publishers, and sheet music that spread well beyond the neighborhood. The Library of Congress collection is unusually rich because many surviving songs can be connected directly to this theater world. ([The Library of Congress][1])
   **Map potential:** very high. Immigration → neighborhood → theaters → publishers → songs spreading outward. It could feel like entering a musical city-within-the-city rather than visiting isolated landmarks.

2. **The Hit-Song Machine — Tin Pan Alley and the Invention of the Music Business, ca. 1890–1915**
   **Narrative question:** *How did New York turn songs into mass-produced hits before records dominated music?* West 28th Street became synonymous with publishers, composers and popular-song promotion; the surviving Tin Pan Alley buildings are now designated landmarks because of that history. ([New York City Government][2])
   The interesting story isn't simply "famous songwriters worked here." It's the **system**: songwriters, publishers, theaters, performers and promoters clustered physically close together. That makes the geography itself explanatory.

3. **Harlem Behind Closed Doors — Rent Parties, Nightclubs and the Economics of Jazz, ca. 1920–1935**
   **Narrative question:** *What if some of New York's important musical spaces weren't clubs at all, but apartments?* NYPL documents rent parties taking place in Harlem homes alongside the neighborhood's clubs and dance halls, while places such as the Cotton Club belonged to a very different, highly commercial nightlife economy. ([libguides.nypl.org][3])
   That gives the route a useful tension: **private versus public Harlem, community space versus entertainment business, and Black performance versus the racial restrictions surrounding parts of the commercial nightlife world.**

4. **When Harlem Met Havana — The New York Making of Latin Jazz and Salsa, ca. 1930s–1970s**
   **Narrative question:** *How did Caribbean music become New York music?* Research on Spanish Harlem points to important interaction between jazz and Afro-Caribbean musicians, while later mambo and salsa developed through New York's Latino communities, clubs, musicians and recording industry. Carnegie Hall and the Smithsonian both treat New York as crucial to salsa's development. ([New York Public Library][4])
   This one has excellent geographic movement: **Harlem/El Barrio → Midtown dance halls → neighborhood streets and clubs → major concert stages.** It could show musical ideas crossing social and geographic boundaries rather than presenting salsa as a straight-line genre history.

5. **The Village Before the Myth — How a Neighborhood Built the Folk Revival, ca. 1955–1965**
   **Narrative question:** *Why did so many folk musicians end up within a few blocks of one another?* The Museum of the City of New York describes Greenwich Village's clubs and coffeehouses as the center of New York's 1950s–60s folk revival, connecting established figures with a new generation of performers. ([Museum of the City of New York][5])
   Rather than making this "the Bob Dylan tour," I'd make it a story about the **ecosystem**: cheap performance spaces, coffeehouses, political activity, audiences, musicians and informal gathering places that allowed a scene to reproduce itself.

6. **Who Built the Modern Dance Floor? — New York's Underground Disco Culture, ca. 1970–1987**
   **Narrative question:** *Before disco became glitter and celebrity, what was happening inside New York's underground dance spaces?* Carnegie Hall's music-history project traces early New York disco through house and loft parties, bathhouses, Black, Latino and gay audiences, DJs and later spaces such as Paradise Garage; it also emphasizes how the DJ became central to shaping the musical experience. ([Timeline of African American Music][6])
   This could be especially strong visually because the story is about **spaces being engineered for sound and dancing**: sound systems, turntables, lighting, record formats, DJs and crowds. The club itself becomes an instrument.

7. **New York's Cultural Collision, 1980–1986**
   **Narrative question:** *What happens when music stops respecting scene boundaries?* MCNY documents an early-'80s environment in which punk, pop, hip-hop, salsa, jazz and experimental music circulated through clubs, bars, theaters, parks and art spaces; its exhibition deliberately used individual musical moments to show that larger ecology. ([Museum of the City of New York][7])
   There's abundant material here, but **I'd currently rank it lower for us** because it gravitates toward the cross-genre/network idea we accidentally reached in the previous attempt. The research supports it; it simply isn't as fresh for this project.

8. **The Moving Music City — Why New York's Music Scenes Keep Changing Address**
   This is the most conceptual candidate. Rather than choosing a genre, ask: **why does New York repeatedly create intense musical clusters, and why do those clusters move or disappear?** Tin Pan Alley followed the entertainment business northward; decades later MCNY describes SoHo becoming increasingly expensive as emerging artists and musicians sought affordable space elsewhere. ([New York City Government][2])
   A route could follow **music and urban change together**: migration, transportation, rents, nightlife regulation, redevelopment and new performance spaces. This needs another research round before I'd trust the full thesis, but it could produce a very distinctive SoundAtlas narrative.

### The three I would investigate first

**#1 Yiddish Broadway** is the biggest discovery from this pass for me. It's geographically concentrated, culturally rich, has excellent primary-source material, and doesn't resemble what we've been working on.

**#4 Harlem → Latin jazz → salsa** probably has the strongest combination of **movement, communities, musical transformation and map storytelling**. The geography could actually demonstrate the argument.

**#6 Underground disco** has perhaps the clearest experiential narrative. Instead of saying "this club was important," we can show **how a new type of musical space was constructed and why that changed the role of records, DJs and dancers.** ([Timeline of African American Music][6])

The important difference from my first attempt is that I wouldn't yet write chapters for any of these. **These are research-derived narrative candidates, not narratives pretending to already be proven.** The next useful step would be to take perhaps three of them and do a quick **evidence + map-potential test**: identify 5–8 candidate events/places for each and see whether a coherent story actually emerges.
