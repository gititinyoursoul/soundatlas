# Narrative Concept Framing

## Goal

Take a promising research topic and explore what kind of story it could support before committing to a route structure, event roster, or final thesis.

The purpose is to identify the strongest **narrative angle** for the topic.

This stage is interpretive and exploratory. It does not yet prove the narrative.

## Inputs

A topic that has already passed basic discovery, for example:

* a historical scene
* a genre
* a community
* a period
* a musical practice
* a place-based cultural development

Ideally, there is already enough initial research to know that the topic is historically significant and plausibly map-worthy.

## Process

### 1. Ask what is actually interesting about the topic

Do not stop at the subject label.

For example:

> Disco in New York

is only a subject.

Concept framing asks:

* What changed?
* What was new?
* What tension existed?
* Who or what drove the change?
* Why does this story matter beyond the genre itself?

The aim is to find the historical mechanism underneath the topic.

### 2. Separate the topic from the possible story

Generate several possible interpretations of the same subject.

A topic might potentially be framed around:

* communities
* technology
* spaces
* migration
* economics
* cultural exchange
* commercialization
* conflict
* infrastructure
* artistic practice
* social change

Do not assume the first plausible interpretation is the best one.

### 3. Identify narrative tensions

Look for contrasts or transformations that could give the story shape.

Examples include:

* underground vs mainstream
* private vs commercial
* local vs citywide
* community-controlled vs industry-controlled
* live performance vs recorded music
* inclusion vs exclusion
* experimentation vs standardization
* emergence vs backlash

These tensions are provisional narrative tools, not yet established conclusions.

### 4. Explore why geography might matter

Consider how the topic could become spatially legible.

Possible geographic mechanisms include:

* movement between neighborhoods
* clustering of venues or businesses
* migration
* changing nightlife districts
* different communities occupying different spaces
* routes through which music, people, records, or ideas circulated
* movement from private to public space
* expansion from local scene to larger market

At this stage, this is only a conceptual map hypothesis.

It still needs to be tested against actual places and evidence.

### 5. Generate possible narrative questions

Turn the topic into one or more questions that could guide later research.

Strong narrative questions usually describe a transformation or relationship.

For example:

> How did a particular type of space change musical practice?

> How did music move between different communities?

> Why did a scene emerge in this part of the city?

> How did a local underground culture become commercially visible?

The question should be specific enough to guide research but open enough for the evidence to challenge it.

### 6. Draft provisional thesis directions

Create several possible answers to the narrative question.

These are hypotheses, not conclusions.

For example:

> The important innovation was not only a new musical style, but a new social space for experiencing music.

A thesis direction is useful because it exposes what the eventual route would need to demonstrate.

If the evidence cannot support it later, it should be revised or abandoned.

### 7. Explore alternative narrative arcs

Sketch different ways the story might develop.

For example:

**Community-centered**

* formation of a community
* creation of spaces
* shared practices
* wider visibility
* pressure or transformation

**Technology-centered**

* existing practice
* new tools or techniques
* experimentation
* adoption
* long-term influence

**Space-centered**

* earlier venue model
* emergence of a new type of space
* spatial or social innovations
* replication
* commercialization or disappearance

These arcs are not yet chapters. They are ways of testing whether the topic contains enough internal movement to sustain a narrative.

### 8. Think in narrative roles rather than famous places

Before researching specific route stops, define the kinds of functions the narrative might require.

Examples:

* context
* emergence
* innovation
* community formation
* transmission
* crossover
* commercialization
* conflict
* backlash
* legacy

This helps prevent the later route from becoming a collection of famous landmarks.

### 9. Identify what makes the concept promising

Summarize why the topic appears suitable for deeper testing.

Consider:

* historical significance
* narrative tension
* geographic relevance
* visual potential
* manageable scope
* distinctiveness from existing routes
* possibility of showing change over time

The result should explain why the idea deserves an evidence test without pretending the route has already been validated.

## Output

A concept-framing result can contain:

* working topic title
* central historical question
* 2–3 possible narrative interpretations
* provisional thesis directions
* important tensions
* possible geographic mechanisms
* alternative narrative arcs
* likely narrative roles
* reasons the topic appears promising

## What this stage should not produce

Concept framing should not yet create:

* a fixed route
* a confirmed event list
* a final chapter structure
* a definitive thesis
* claims presented as proven
* a list of famous venues used as assumed route stops

Those belong after the concept has been tested against evidence.

## Stop Condition

Concept framing is complete when there is at least one clear, testable narrative hypothesis and a plausible idea of how geography could contribute to the story.

The next stage should then be a lightweight **evidence + map-potential test**.

That test determines whether the concept survives contact with actual sources, events, places, chronology, and geography.

## Principle

The concept-framing stage asks:

> **What might the story be?**

The evidence-testing stage asks:

> **Does the historical record actually support that story?**

Keeping those stages separate reduces the risk of selecting a narrative first and then searching only for evidence that confirms it.
