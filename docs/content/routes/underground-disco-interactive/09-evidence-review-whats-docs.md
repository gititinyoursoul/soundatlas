# Narrative Evidence Review

## Goal

Audit the drafted route narrative against the underlying research before treating the copy as publication-ready.

This stage verifies that the prose does not overstate what the evidence can support.

Its purpose is to identify:

* directly supported claims
* justified synthesis
* weak or ambiguous wording
* unsupported causality
* claims requiring corroboration
* details that should be removed or narrowed

The output is a corrected narrative draft plus an explicit evidence-quality assessment.

## Inputs

A narrative draft based on:

* a stable route cohort
* deep-researched event dossiers
* cross-stop synthesis
* a working thesis
* defined section and transition logic

The draft should already reflect the intended route structure.

## Core Principle

The evidence review should audit the prose that was actually written.

Do not merely ask whether each route event is generally well researched.

Ask:

> **Does every important sentence say only what the available evidence justifies?**

A historically defensible route can still contain unsupported wording if the narrative draft introduces stronger claims than the research dossiers established.

## Process

### 1. Audit the overall thesis

Start with the route-level argument.

Break the thesis into its component claims.

For example, a thesis may contain claims about:

* multiple communities
* a historical transformation
* technological change
* geographic movement
* cultural exchange
* institutional development

Check whether every major element appears somewhere in the researched cohort.

If the thesis is broader than the events can demonstrate, narrow the thesis rather than stretching the evidence.

### 2. Review each event section claim by claim

For every event, identify the factual and interpretive claims in the prose.

Useful categories include:

**Directly supported**

The source explicitly establishes the claim.

**Supported synthesis**

The claim is an interpretation reasonably derived from multiple supported facts.

**Needs softer wording**

The evidence supports the general direction but not the certainty or scope of the sentence.

**Needs corroboration**

The claim depends too heavily on one source, retrospective testimony, or weak evidence.

**Unsupported**

The available research does not establish the claim.

**Remove**

The detail adds little narrative value and cannot be defended reliably.

### 3. Audit vivid narrative details carefully

Drafting often introduces details that make the story more engaging but were not explicitly established during research.

Examples:

* descriptions of what a room looked like
* claims about what audiences felt
* statements about what was or was not physically present
* dramatic descriptions of crowd behavior
* exact motivations of historical actors

If these details are not sourced, remove or qualify them.

Narrative vividness should not depend on invention.

### 4. Review origin and superlative claims

Search explicitly for words and phrases such as:

* first
* invented
* birthplace
* originated
* created
* pioneered
* transformed everything
* most important
* unprecedented

These claims require unusually strong evidence.

If the evidence is weaker, replace them with bounded formulations such as:

* became an important example
* helped develop
* contributed to
* became an incubator
* represented one influential model
* was among the spaces where the practice developed

### 5. Verify event boundaries in the prose

Make sure the narrative does not accidentally broaden an event beyond its researched scope.

For example:

Research may establish:

> A community center became an important incubator in 1974.

The draft should not silently become:

> The practice was invented at this community center.

Likewise:

Research:

> A venue was operating by late 1974.

Draft:

> The venue opened on an exact date.

The prose must preserve the precision and uncertainty of the dossier.

### 6. Audit causal language

Transitions are especially vulnerable to unsupported causality.

Look for language such as:

* led to
* caused
* inspired
* produced
* resulted in
* paved the way for
* evolved into

Ask whether the relationship is actually documented.

If not, use wording that reflects the real relationship:

**Parallel development**

> At the same time, another scene was developing...

**Narrative comparison**

> A different version of the same broader change appeared...

**Later convergence**

> By the end of the decade, several of these characteristics could be seen together...

**Structural relationship**

> Both participated in a wider network...

Do not turn chronological proximity into causation.

### 7. Review cross-stop transitions

For every transition between stops or sections, ask:

> Why is the narrative moving here?

A transition may be justified by:

* documented influence
* a change in scale
* parallel development
* geographic contrast
* creation of infrastructure
* convergence
* chronological progression

Make the transition logic explicit without implying relationships that have not been demonstrated.

### 8. Distinguish historical fact from narrative synthesis

Some of the most valuable statements in a route are interpretive.

For example:

> These events reveal several communities changing nightlife in different ways.

That may not appear verbatim in any source.

It can still be defensible if each component is supported.

Such statements should be treated as **supported synthesis**, not as directly sourced historical fact.

This distinction allows interpretation without disguising it as documentary evidence.

### 9. Audit representation claims

Check whether the narrative accurately describes communities and historical agency.

Avoid phrases that:

* treat a community as homogeneous
* turn audience presence into evidence of ownership or innovation
* assign one venue to an entire community
* imply that one event represents the complete experience of a group

Prefer precise descriptions of what actors actually did:

* promoted
* owned
* danced
* organized
* performed
* designed
* distributed
* selected
* engineered

### 10. Strengthen weak claims where possible

When an important claim is weak but route-relevant, perform focused corroboration rather than broad research.

Good targets include:

* contemporary newspaper coverage
* trade publications
* archival ephemera
* business records
* institutional collections
* period advertisements
* independent oral histories

The purpose is to resolve the exact claim under review.

### 11. Separate event viability from claim viability

A weak claim does not automatically make an event unusable.

For example:

An event may be strongly established, while a claim that it was “the first of its kind” remains uncertain.

In that case:

* retain the event
* remove or soften the superlative

This prevents unnecessary route restructuring.

### 12. Rewrite the draft during the audit

Do not produce only a list of problems.

Create a corrected version in which:

* unsupported details are removed
* uncertain claims are qualified
* transitions are made evidence-aware
* causality is corrected
* synthesis is clearly framed
* the thesis matches the evidence

The result should be closer to publication-ready copy.

## Suggested Evidence-Audit Table

| Draft area         | Classification         | Problem               | Action                          |
| ------------------ | ---------------------- | --------------------- | ------------------------------- |
| Overall thesis     | Supported synthesis    | Slightly too broad    | Narrow wording                  |
| Event A            | Directly supported     | —                     | Keep                            |
| Event B            | Needs corroboration    | Exact opening claim   | Remove exact date               |
| Event C            | Strong after narrowing | Draft implies origin  | Reframe as incubator            |
| Transition A → B   | Unsupported causality  | No influence evidence | Present as parallel development |
| Event D conclusion | Supported synthesis    | Interpretive          | Keep with careful phrasing      |

## Claim-Level Confidence

A useful internal confidence model is:

**High**

* multiple reliable sources
* contemporary or primary corroboration
* little ambiguity

**Medium**

* event is well established
* one important detail or interpretation remains uncertain

**Low**

* claim depends on one weak source
* major factual uncertainty
* unsupported superlative or causal statement

Low-confidence claims should generally not appear as definitive statements in final copy.

## Output

A completed narrative evidence review should produce:

* route-level thesis audit
* claim-level assessment for each event
* transition audit
* identification of unsupported causality
* identification of risky origin or superlative claims
* focused corroboration where necessary
* a corrected narrative draft
* explicit remaining claim-level uncertainties
* readiness verdict

## Possible Verdicts

### Ready to advance

No structural problems remain.

Remaining uncertainties are minor or can be handled through wording.

### Ready with claim-level caveats

The narrative works, but a small number of claims must remain qualified or omitted.

### Focused corroboration required

One or more important claims need targeted source work before publication.

### Return to synthesis

The evidence review reveals that the narrative structure itself depends on unsupported relationships.

### Reopen cohort

Only when the audit shows that a core event cannot support its assigned route function.

This should be relatively rare if earlier stages were completed properly.

## Stop Condition

The evidence review is complete when:

1. every major factual claim has a defensible source basis;
2. interpretive claims can be traced back to supported evidence;
3. unsupported causal relationships have been removed;
4. origin and superlative claims are either strongly supported or qualified;
5. event boundaries remain consistent with the research dossiers;
6. remaining uncertainties are explicit;
7. no unresolved issue is likely to change the route structure.

The route can then move into **final copy and presentation treatment**.

## Principle

Narrative drafting asks:

> **How do we tell the story?**

Evidence review asks:

> **Does the story we wrote still match what the research actually established?**

The aim is not to remove interpretation.

It is to ensure that interpretation remains distinguishable from evidence and that compelling prose never becomes stronger than the historical record.
