# Light Evidence + Route-Shape Test

## Goal

Test whether a promising narrative concept can be supported by actual historical evidence and whether it produces a meaningful map-based route.

This stage is deliberately lightweight.

It should be strong enough to reject, reshape, or advance a concept without yet performing full route research.

## Inputs

A concept-framing result containing at least:

* a working topic
* a provisional narrative question
* one or more thesis directions
* possible narrative tensions
* an initial idea of how geography might matter

These are hypotheses to test, not conclusions to confirm.

## Core Principle

Do not search for evidence merely to prove the concept.

Use evidence to determine whether the concept should:

* survive
* change
* narrow
* broaden
* or be rejected

The test is successful even when it disproves the original framing.

## Process

### 1. State the hypothesis being tested

Begin with one provisional narrative question or thesis.

For example:

> How did a particular kind of music space change the way music was experienced?

The question should be specific enough to guide candidate selection but flexible enough to change after research.

### 2. Build a minimum provisional candidate cohort

Identify a small number of plausible events, places, people-place relationships, or historical transitions.

A useful starting range is approximately 5–8 candidates.

The cohort should cover different possible functions in the narrative rather than simply collect the most famous landmarks.

Possible roles include:

* context
* emergence
* innovation
* community formation
* transmission
* divergence
* commercialization
* conflict
* culmination
* legacy

The cohort is provisional. Inclusion at this stage does not mean inclusion in the final route.

### 3. Gather only minimum sufficient evidence

For each candidate, collect enough evidence to answer:

* What happened?
* When did it happen?
* Where did it happen?
* Who was involved?
* Why might it matter to the narrative?
* Is there a credible source supporting the claim?

Avoid exhaustive research at this stage.

The aim is to establish whether the candidate has a defensible historical identity and route function.

### 4. Assign a provisional narrative role

For each candidate, describe what work it might perform in the story.

For example:

> foundational model

> technological innovation

> commercialization

> contrasting branch

> community context

> later culmination

This helps distinguish historically important places from places that are actually necessary to the route.

### 5. Make an initial inclusion call

Give each candidate a lightweight status, such as:

* **Core include**
* **Include**
* **Context**
* **Tentative**
* **Scope-dependent**
* **Exclude**

This is not a final editorial decision.

It is a way to see whether the route already has a coherent minimum structure.

### 6. Plot the provisional cohort

Place the candidates on a map.

Then ask what the geography reveals.

Look for:

* clusters
* movement
* neighborhood contrasts
* uptown/downtown relationships
* community boundaries
* changing venue types
* expansion or contraction
* multiple historical processes occupying the same area
* surprising proximity between otherwise different scenes

The map should be used analytically, not just as presentation.

### 7. Look for spatially meaningful relationships

Individual pins are less important than relationships between them.

Examples:

* two competing spaces in the same building
* a cultural practice moving between neighborhoods
* a scene shifting toward a commercial district
* a peripheral location becoming a new center
* several different communities using nearby spaces differently

These relationships often reveal stronger narrative structure than chronology alone.

### 8. Test the route shape

Ask whether the candidate cohort forms a meaningful progression.

Possible shapes include:

**Linear transformation**

* emergence
* development
* expansion
* decline

**Branching**

* shared origin
* different interpretations
* competing outcomes

**Network**

* multiple places exchanging people, ideas, technology, or music

**Contrast**

* two or more simultaneous versions of the same cultural practice

**Spatial migration**

* the scene repeatedly moves as social or economic conditions change

Do not force the route into a simple chronological line if the evidence suggests another structure.

### 9. Compare the evidence to the original concept

Ask:

* Does the evidence actually support the thesis?
* Is the route overrepresenting one community, neighborhood, or institution?
* Are important groups or processes missing?
* Does the evidence suggest a different historical mechanism?
* Is the proposed timeframe still appropriate?
* Are some presumed key places weaker than expected?
* Has a more interesting story emerged?

This is one of the most important steps.

### 10. Revise the working thesis

If necessary, rewrite the narrative hypothesis based on what the candidate evidence shows.

For example:

Initial hypothesis:

> A local underground scene became mainstream.

Evidence may instead suggest:

> Several competing models emerged from the same underground scene.

The second thesis should replace the first if it better explains the evidence.

### 11. Identify evidence gaps

Record what the provisional cohort cannot yet demonstrate.

Examples:

* a community named in the thesis is poorly represented
* an important geographic area has no candidates
* a key transition lacks a defensible event
* the route relies too heavily on one type of source
* the chronology jumps over an important phase

These gaps define the next research task.

Do not hide them by stretching existing candidates beyond what their evidence supports.

### 12. Give a route-potential verdict

At the end of the test, assign an overall decision.

Possible outcomes:

**Advance**

The concept has defensible evidence and meaningful geographic structure.

**Advance, but reshape**

The topic is strong, but the thesis, scope, candidate cohort, or route structure should change.

**Research gap**

The concept remains plausible, but a specific missing area must be investigated before deciding.

**Reject**

The topic may be historically interesting, but the evidence or geography does not support a strong route.

## Suggested Candidate Test Format

For each provisional candidate, record:

| Field            | Purpose                                |
| ---------------- | -------------------------------------- |
| Candidate        | Event, place, or historical transition |
| Date             | Approximate or exact timeframe         |
| Location         | Mappable place                         |
| Minimal evidence | What the source establishes            |
| Narrative role   | Why it might belong                    |
| Map value        | What geography contributes             |
| Initial call     | Include, tentative, context, etc.      |
| Sources          | Evidence supporting the candidate      |

This should remain compact.

It is a test instrument, not the final route dossier.

## Output

A completed light evidence + route-shape test should produce:

* the hypothesis tested
* approximately 5–8 provisional candidates
* minimal evidence for each
* candidate narrative roles
* initial inclusion calls
* a plotted geographic cohort
* observations about spatial relationships
* an emerging route structure
* weaknesses or representation gaps
* a revised thesis if necessary
* an overall advance/reshape/reject verdict
* a focused recommendation for the next research step

## What This Stage Should Not Do

Do not yet:

* fully research every candidate
* finalize route stops
* write polished chapters
* create final event descriptions
* force balanced representation without evidence
* assume famous places must remain
* turn contextual conditions automatically into route events
* treat the provisional thesis as established fact

## Stop Condition

The test is complete when there is enough evidence to answer two questions:

> **Is there a defensible historical story here?**

and

> **Does geography materially improve that story?**

If both answers are yes, the topic can advance.

If the answers are partially yes, the next research task should target the specific weakness revealed by the test rather than restarting broad discovery.

## Principle

Concept framing asks:

> **What might the story be?**

The light evidence + route-shape test asks:

> **What story begins to emerge when a small set of real places and evidence is placed on the map?**

Full route research comes only after that answer is strong enough to justify deeper work.
