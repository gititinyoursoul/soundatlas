I think the cohort can now be **provisionally locked for deep research**. The minimum-sufficient test leaves us with **six stops**: each performs a different job, and removing any one creates a clear hole in the story.

## Hypothesis being tested

The strongest version now seems to be:

> **New York disco developed through overlapping experiments in what a dance floor could be. Private parties, Black-owned clubs, Puerto Rican social-dance culture, DJ-led venues, and new record-distribution networks each changed how recorded music, dancers, technology, and community interacted.**

This is somewhat narrower—and stronger—than saying that all of disco simply emerged from one underground scene.

## Can anything be removed?

This is where the test becomes useful.

**The Loft cannot be removed.** Without it, we lose the early private-party model and an evidenced connection to later clubs including the Gallery and Paradise Garage. ([NYC LGBTQ Historic Sites][1])

**Leviticus cannot be removed** without pushing the story back toward a predominantly downtown queer-club genealogy. More importantly, the Smithsonian archive shows that Black-oriented disco venues were a substantial historical world of their own rather than merely an audience within the familiar downtown clubs. ([sova.si.edu][7])

**St. Mary's cannot be removed** if we want to claim that different communities constructed disco culture in different ways. It is the only current stop where the primary historical actor is the **dancer/community**, rather than promoter, DJ or club owner. ([The Bronx County Historical Society][3])

**The Gallery is the closest thing to a cut candidate.** The Loft and Paradise Garage could theoretically carry the DJ/sound story without it. But then we'd jump from a 1970 house party almost directly to a 1977 purpose-built club. The Gallery provides evidence for the **transformation between those forms**, so I would retain it. ([NYC LGBTQ Historic Sites][4])

**The Record Pool could be folded into the Loft**, because 99 Prince Street was also the Loft's second home. But conceptually it performs a completely different job: it shows that the culture was becoming infrastructure, not merely producing more venues. I therefore think it deserves its own event. ([NYC LGBTQ Historic Sites][1])

**Paradise Garage cannot be removed.** It provides the strongest later point where community, DJ selection, sound-system design, extended dancing and influence on record circulation become visible together. ([NYC LGBTQ Historic Sites][6])

So **six looks genuinely minimum-sufficient**, rather than six being an arbitrary target.

## What gets cut

Several previous candidates now become unnecessary.

**Studio 54 → context, not stop.** It's valuable for explaining disco's celebrity/mainstream image, but our route is no longer fundamentally about underground → mainstream commercialization. Adding it would pull the story toward a familiar narrative without filling a necessary gap.

**Continental Baths → context.** Useful prehistory for gay nightlife, but the Loft gives us a stronger foundational event for this particular thesis.

**Flamingo → reserve.** Its contrast with the Gallery is interesting, but no longer necessary to the minimum argument.

**The Saint → epilogue/reserve.** Excellent for a later story about technologically engineered clubs, but Paradise Garage already gives us a strong culmination.

**Ginza / La Martinique → context inside the Leviticus event.** They explain how Best of Friends began promoting parties before moving into ownership; making all three separate stops would overrepresent one network. Medgar Evers College's account explicitly traces that progression. ([Medgar Evers College -][2])

## Route shape

I don't think this should become six chronological chapters of equal weight.

A more meaningful structure is:

**I. A different kind of dance space appears**
**The Loft**

↓

**II. Different New Yorkers begin remaking the dance floor in parallel**

**Leviticus** → promoters, ownership, Black nightlife economy
**St. Mary's** → Puerto Rican dancers, neighborhood practice
**The Gallery** → DJs, sound, lighting, queer/POC club culture

↓

**III. Those practices become a connected system**

**New York Record Pool** → records circulate between labels and DJs

↓

**IV. The pieces converge**

**Paradise Garage** → DJ + sound + dancers + community + record influence

This is important because the route no longer implies:

> Loft invented disco → everybody copied it.

Instead, the Loft is **one foundational strand**, while Black entrepreneurship and Bronx Latin dance culture introduce historical processes that aren't descendants of the Loft in the same simple way. The Smithsonian material strongly supports resisting a single downtown genealogy. ([sova.si.edu][7])

## Geography test: passes

The map now makes a historical argument.

There is a **downtown Manhattan cluster** around the Loft, Gallery, Record Pool and Paradise Garage. But Leviticus pulls the story into Midtown's Black professional nightlife, while St. Mary's creates a substantial South Bronx node. ([NYC LGBTQ Historic Sites][1])

That spatial separation reinforces the thesis: **the New York disco dance floor wasn't being constructed in a single neighborhood or social scene.**

And then 99 Prince Street and Paradise Garage help show increasing connectivity between those worlds through DJs, records and shared dance-floor practices. That last point is partly an interpretive inference from the candidate evidence rather than a claim that every community literally passed through those two sites. ([timlawrence.info][5])

## Coverage test

The six-stop cohort gives us:

**Chronology:** 1970 → 1978 ✔
**Private-party culture:** Loft ✔
**Black club entrepreneurship:** Leviticus/TBOF ✔
**Puerto Rican/Bronx contribution:** St. Mary's ✔
**Queer and people-of-color club culture:** Loft, Gallery, Garage ✔
**Dancer agency:** St. Mary's ✔
**DJ/sound-space development:** Loft → Gallery → Garage ✔
**Music-distribution infrastructure:** Record Pool ✔
**Meaningful geography:** Bronx + Midtown + downtown Manhattan ✔

One thing it **doesn't** provide is broad borough coverage. The Best of Friends network reached Queens and Brooklyn, but our minimum route does not require separate stops there. That seems acceptable: borough diversity shouldn't become a quota if those additional stops don't perform unique narrative work. The TBOF history can establish that wider network inside the Leviticus story. ([Medgar Evers College -][2])

## Revised provisional thesis

I'd lock this for the next research stage:

> **Disco's New York dance floor was not invented in a single club. During the 1970s, different communities experimented with new relationships between records, DJs, dancers, sound systems and social space. Private parties created new forms of collective listening; Black promoters built their own nightlife networks; Puerto Rican dancers transformed social dancing in the Bronx; DJs turned clubs into responsive musical environments; and record pools connected those spaces to the recording industry. By the time Paradise Garage emerged, these experiments had helped establish a new model of dance culture.**

The word **“helped”** matters. At this evidence level I wouldn't yet claim these six places alone *created modern club culture*. That's a larger thesis requiring deeper comparative evidence.

### Verdict: **cohort passes — provisionally lock these six**

The next step should therefore **stop candidate discovery** and switch modes.

We should deep-research these six events individually: establish precise event boundaries, dates, addresses, actors, claims, primary/secondary sources, source URLs, uncertainties, useful archival material, and exactly what each event can legitimately demonstrate.

Only after that should we write the actual route narrative.
