## Evidence boundary

This is newly sourced concept research, not a regenerated legacy route. The
Sources below establish viable historical directions, archives, actors, places,
and risks; they do not yet prove an Event cohort or final thesis. The
implementing agent inspected the legacy/v2 and canonical surfaces before this
run to establish workflow state and the exclusion boundary, so this is not a
blinded comparison. No excluded artifact is cited as evidence or copied as an
answer here; the claims and recommendations below are re-derived from the named
fresh Sources.

The strongest fresh evidence anchors are:

- Carnegie Hall describes early US Disco as emerging through underground New
  York house and loft parties and bathhouses used by African American, Latino,
  and gay communities, and separately describes the role of record pools and
  club DJs in bypassing established radio promotion and building a market for
  remixes and 12-inch singles.
- The Smithsonian's New York City Disco Clubs Collection contains 1971–1982
  club and party ephemera, concentrated in 1972–1975 and particularly in venues
  owned by or catering to Black New Yorkers. Its catalog exposes a larger
  nightlife landscape than the best-known downtown rooms.
- The Bronx County Historical Society documents Latin Hustle developing among
  Puerto Rican teenagers in the South Bronx, including house-party,
  recreation-center, local-club, and downtown movement.
- The NYC LGBTQ Historic Sites Project documents the Loft and Gallery as
  multiracial, queer-centered spaces in which room design, sound, lighting,
  records, DJs, and dancers were organized together; it also documents the
  Paradise Garage's later musical and community role.
- NYU's Paradise Garage archive preserves photographs, flyers, tickets, club
  rules, record-company material, and evidence of the transition from Disco to
  DJ-driven house music. Rights for reproduction are not supplied by archive
  access and remain unassessed.

## Competing arcs

### Arc A — Black Disco New York: a citywide nightlife ecosystem

- Historical question: What becomes visible when New York Disco is mapped
  through Black-owned and Black-oriented clubs, promoters, parties, audiences,
  and artifacts rather than through a small inherited club canon?
- Tension: A large documented nightlife landscape survives in ephemera, yet
  later histories and readily accessible venue narratives may concentrate
  attention on a few downtown rooms.
- Provisional thesis: Black New Yorkers did not merely participate in a
  pre-existing Disco scene; clubs, promoters, DJs, audiences, and social
  networks formed a substantial nightlife ecosystem with its own geography and
  infrastructure.
- Likely actors: club owners and promoters, DJs, dancers, venue staff,
  photographers and designers of ephemera, and Black nightlife audiences.
- Geographic hypothesis: The Smithsonian collection may reveal connections
  among Manhattan, Bronx, outer-borough, hotel, and nearby metropolitan venues
  that differ from a downtown-only map.
- Probable route shape: A network of parties, rooms, promotional circulation,
  and audience movement rather than one venue genealogy.
- What it may hide: Queer-specific organizing, Puerto Rican dancer agency,
  sound-system practice, and the musical feedback between clubs and records
  unless Candidates expose those responsibilities directly.
- Main risks: The online collection descriptions may support existence and
  audience orientation without supplying enough event-level dates, ownership,
  actors, or historical mechanisms. “Black-oriented” must not be converted
  automatically into Black ownership or homogeneous community identity.
- Disconfirming evidence to seek: Whether the collection yields enough exact
  New York Event boundaries and claim-specific records for a coherent route;
  whether apparently separate venues were connected; and whether the network
  interpretation survives deeper archival inspection.

### Arc B — Dancers make the scene

- Historical question: How did dancers and community spaces change what Disco
  was, rather than merely respond to music chosen by clubs and DJs?
- Tension: Standard accounts can make the DJ, venue, or record the active
  subject while rendering dancers as an anonymous crowd.
- Provisional thesis: Dancers developed practices, standards, and forms of
  social organization that moved among homes, recreation centers, local clubs,
  and larger nightlife spaces, making the dance floor an engine of cultural
  invention.
- Likely actors: Puerto Rican Hustle dancers and organizers in the Bronx,
  dancers in Black and queer nightlife, community-center staff, dance crews,
  teachers, club audiences, and musicians whose records supported those
  practices.
- Geographic hypothesis: The route could connect South Bronx house parties and
  St. Mary's Recreation Center to local clubs and selective Manhattan venues,
  making movement between community and commercial spaces part of the argument.
- Probable route shape: A practice-centered route in which spaces matter
  because they host learning, competition, safety, performance, or
  transmission—not because every practice has one birthplace.
- What it may hide: Record-pool infrastructure, production, sound design, and
  Black nightlife beyond the currently strong Latin Hustle evidence.
- Main risks: Dance origins are especially vulnerable to retrospective credit,
  community flattening, and false precision. The strongest fresh dancer
  evidence currently supports Latin Hustle; comparable New York evidence for
  other dancer communities is an Evidence Gap.
- Disconfirming evidence to seek: Whether at least five distinct, mappable
  New York moments can be supported without turning practices into invented
  point-origins; whether dancers' own testimony supports the proposed movement;
  and whether the route remains music history rather than a generic dance
  survey.

### Arc C — The dance-floor feedback loop

- Historical question: How did dancers' responses become information that
  moved through DJs, record pools, labels, stores, radio, remixes, and new
  records before returning to the floor?
- Tension: Music-business histories can treat consumers as an endpoint, while
  club histories can treat records as a finished input.
- Provisional thesis: New York Disco created a feedback system in which club
  response helped DJs promote, test, extend, and recirculate records, shifting
  how dance music was produced and marketed.
- Likely actors: dancers, club DJs, record-pool organizers, remixers, label and
  record-store workers, radio programmers, producers, musicians, and performers.
- Geographic hypothesis: Clubs, record pools, record stores, labels, studios,
  and radio form a network map whose edges matter more than a walking sequence.
- Probable route shape: Follow a record or a set of linked decisions through
  floor response, DJ selection, pooled distribution, remix or 12-inch format,
  retail/radio uptake, and return to dancers.
- What it may hide: Community life, ownership and exclusion, embodied dance,
  and spaces whose importance did not produce a traceable commercial record.
- Main risks: The broad feedback mechanism is well supported, but exact New
  York transactions and causal sequences need event-level trade or archival
  evidence. A diagram could imply closed-loop causality where only parallel
  influence is documented.
- Disconfirming evidence to seek: Whether specific records, pools, stores,
  labels, and clubs can be linked with dates and supported influence; whether
  the map adds historical understanding; and whether the route can retain
  human characters rather than becoming an industry schematic.

### Arc D — The room becomes an instrument

- Historical question: How did New Yorkers design parties and clubs so that
  sound, lighting, records, DJs, architecture, and dancers operated as one
  musical environment?
- Tension: A club may appear to be a container for music even when its physical
  and social design changes how music can be heard and performed.
- Provisional thesis: Underground Disco developed through experiments in the
  whole room: the social rules, sound system, DJ practice, lighting, floor, and
  audience became interdependent parts of collective musical experience.
- Likely actors: David Mancuso, Nicky Siano, Larry Levan, sound designers such
  as Alex Rosner and Richard Long, club organizers, dancers, performers, and
  membership communities.
- Geographic hypothesis: A small set of Manhattan rooms may support a clear
  place sequence, but the route should test whether influence is documented or
  only inferred from chronological resemblance.
- Probable route shape: Compare successive room experiments and the different
  social purposes they served rather than presenting inevitable technical
  progress.
- What it may hide: Black-owned nightlife beyond the canonical rooms, Bronx
  dance practice, record-industry infrastructure, and activities that occurred
  in less formally engineered spaces.
- Main risks: This is the arc most likely to reproduce a familiar venue canon
  and documentation bias. It could mistake well-preserved equipment histories
  for the whole history of Disco.
- Disconfirming evidence to seek: Whether the claimed technical or personnel
  links are documented; whether room design changed musical or community
  practice rather than only spectacle; and whether noncanonical rooms alter the
  sequence materially.

### Arc E — Many New Yorks become “Disco”

- Historical question: How did distinct Black, Latino, queer, local,
  commercial, and media worlds become compressed into one public category of
  Disco?
- Tension: The word “Disco” can make heterogeneous scenes appear unified and
  can replace community-specific meanings with a mass-market image.
- Provisional thesis: New York's Disco boom joined and obscured several
  overlapping dance cultures; the public category expanded through records,
  film, radio, clubs, and fashion while leaving unequal visibility in its wake.
- Likely actors: dancers and organizers from distinct nightlife communities,
  DJs, musicians, record and media workers, club owners, journalists, and mass
  audiences.
- Geographic hypothesis: Contrasting maps—community spaces and commercial or
  media nodes—could show plurality and later concentration without implying
  that every scene converged in the same way.
- Probable route shape: Parallel local developments followed by selective
  commercial amplification and contested public meaning.
- What it may hide: The precise mechanism inside any one community and the
  continued underground life that did not converge into a mainstream boom.
- Main risks: The scope may become an umbrella survey. “Many New Yorks” is not
  a thesis unless evidence identifies exact interactions, asymmetries, and
  consequences.
- Disconfirming evidence to seek: Whether a bounded 5–8 Candidate landscape can
  represent plurality without tokenism; which connections are documented; and
  whether the mass-market turn can be shown through New York-specific Events.

### Arc F — Underground continuity: from Disco to house, care, and survival

- Historical question: How did an underground Disco community continue and
  change through new musical practices, the early AIDS crisis, benefit
  organizing, loss, and later house culture?
- Tension: Narratives of Disco's commercial decline can imply an ending while
  underground rooms and communities continued to produce music and mutual aid.
- Provisional thesis: At spaces such as Paradise Garage, Disco's underground
  practices did not simply disappear; DJ-driven music changed, community
  institutions responded to crisis, and the scene carried ideas into house
  culture.
- Likely actors: Larry Levan, Michael Brody, Mel Cheren, Paradise Garage
  members and performers, GMHC organizers, record-company workers, dancers,
  and later dance-music communities.
- Geographic hypothesis: A tighter lower-Manhattan core may connect the Garage
  to record and benefit-organizing nodes, with external influence treated as a
  relationship rather than an unsupported New York map stop.
- Probable route shape: A 1977–1987 institutional and musical history centered
  on one community anchor and the networks around it.
- What it may hide: Early-1970s formation, Bronx and Black-nightlife breadth,
  and the many communities not represented by one institution.
- Main risks: Centering one famous club may turn the route into an institutional
  biography. AIDS history and community identity require especially careful
  Source and Human judgment. “Disco became house” would be an unacceptable
  simple lineage.
- Disconfirming evidence to seek: Whether several independent Events—not only
  one club timeline—support the route; whether musical transitions and benefit
  organizing can be linked without false causality; and whether the route can
  honor loss without using crisis as narrative atmosphere.

## Recommendation

### Recommended default: compare Arc A and Arc C in step 03

This pair offers the clearest test of whether the new method produces a route
that differs structurally from a familiar venue genealogy:

- Arc A asks whether archival evidence supports a citywide Black-nightlife
  network with actors and places that standard accounts underrepresent.
- Arc C asks whether the music itself can drive a mappable feedback mechanism
  among dancers, DJs, records, pools, labels, stores, and radio.
- A shared 5–8 Candidate landscape can test where these arcs overlap—for
  example, when a Black-oriented club, promoter, DJ, or record node participates
  in circulation—without building two separate cohorts.

### Optional third: Arc B

Add Arc B if the Human wants the light test to require dancer agency and Bronx
community-space evidence as an explicit alternative, accepting that the fresh
Source base is currently uneven beyond Latin Hustle. This is the strongest
protection against treating audiences only as market signals inside Arc C.

Arc D is the best fallback if the recommended comparison cannot establish
event-level evidence. Its Source base and experiential logic are strong, but it
is also the direction most exposed to inherited canon and Manhattan-documentation
bias. Arcs E and F should remain retained alternatives rather than enter the
first light test: E is too broad until specific interactions are found, and F
needs a narrower, historically sensitive run centered on 1977–1987.

## Unknowns and Evidence Gaps

- The Smithsonian catalog's online description does not yet establish enough
  event-level ownership, promoter, date, address, or relationship evidence for
  Arc A. Stage 03 must test exact records rather than infer a network from
  catalog breadth.
- Arc B currently has strong institutional evidence for Latin Hustle but not a
  comparably bounded set of New York dancer-centered practices across the full
  topic. That gap may narrow or disconfirm the arc.
- Arc C's general feedback mechanism is well supported, but specific New York
  causal links among a floor response, DJ, pool, label, store, radio, and record
  remain untested.
- Media availability is visible in several archives, but Event relevance,
  attribution, stable access, accessibility, and rights are `not-assessed`.
- No arc has a Human decision. None is an accepted thesis or route direction.
