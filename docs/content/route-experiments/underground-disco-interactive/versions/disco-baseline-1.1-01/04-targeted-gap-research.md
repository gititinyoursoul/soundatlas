## Research boundary

This stage investigated only the eight material questions named by step 03. It
looked for contemporary trade reporting, institutional collections, direct
participant testimony, contrary evidence, non-venue infrastructure, movement,
and honest map relationships. It did not survey the whole Disco canon or use
the excluded legacy, v2, or canonical Disco route content as an answer.

The search treated participation, ownership, promotion, dancing, DJ practice,
and label decision-making as different kinds of agency. A club receiving free
records or hosting a label party is not by itself evidence that floor response
changed a release. Likewise, shared ownership and geographic proximity do not
by themselves prove that two venues exchanged audiences, records, or practices.

## Gap questions and findings

### 1. Can the Best of Friends network receive exact Queens or Brooklyn places?

Partly resolved. A contemporary Black New York magazine profile identifies
four venues owned by Best of Friends in 1977–78: Lucifer's at 216-19 Linden
Boulevard in Queens; Leviticus at 45 West 33rd Street and Othello at 500 Eighth
Avenue in Manhattan; and Orpheus at 1055 Washington Avenue in Brooklyn. It
also says the group began in 1966 by sponsoring social affairs in Queens.

This establishes a multi-borough ownership network and honest component place
references. It does not yet establish one bounded opening, promotion, audience
movement, or distinct route function for Lucifer's or Orpheus. They therefore
remain components of `best-of-friends-promotion-network`, not newly promoted
Event Candidates.

- Source: [Apple ROUTES, June 1978 — “Entertainment Complexes”](https://routes-mag.com/issue-1978-6-17/)
  supports the contemporary ownership, partner, address, and organizational
  history claims.
- Remaining gap: opening dates, operating boundaries, audience evidence, and
  venue-specific mechanisms for Lucifer's, Othello, and Orpheus are `unknown`.

### 2. Can the Leviticus opening conflict be resolved?

Resolved enough for cohort comparison, with the conflict preserved. Best of
Friends partner Noel Hankin gives Thursday, November 14, 1974 as the public
opening. A contemporary Cash Box item documents a Buddah Group event at
Leviticus by December 1974, independently establishing that the club was open
before 1975. Billboard's March 1976 statement that it opened in November 1975
therefore cannot be used as the opening year; whether it describes a reopening
or is simply an error remains `unknown`.

- [Noel Hankin — “Leviticus Opened 50 Years Ago”](https://www.linkedin.com/pulse/leviticus-opened-50-years-ago-noel-hankin-clh9e)
  supports the exact date as a first-person partner account.
- [Cash Box, December 28, 1974](https://retrocdn.net/images/5/5c/CashBox_US_1974-12-28.pdf)
  independently supports operation and a label event by December 1974.
- [Billboard, March 6, 1976 — “Record Firms Aid Black Disco”](https://www.worldradiohistory.com/Archive-All-Music/Billboard/70s/1976/Billboard%201976-03-06a.pdf)
  supports ownership and recurring label parties but is contrary evidence on
  the year.

The exact day remains medium-confidence rather than settled publication copy
until a contemporary license, opening notice, or newspaper account is found.

### 3. Is there independent dancer-centered evidence?

Resolved enough to keep Arc B as a lens, not enough to restore it as an equal
route arc. The Bronx County Historical Society documents Rocking at St. Mary's
alongside Hustle, names Rock Masters and competitive dancers, and records its
movement toward Manhattan clubs. Its separate Burning account names Black
Bronx dancers and describes how DJs selected and extended percussive breaks in
response to their practice. These are important counterexamples to a story in
which record-industry infrastructure organizes all musical change.

However, the Rocking evidence shares St. Mary's and the same institutional
exhibit as the Latin Hustle Candidate. Treating it as a separate stop would
inflate one documentary cluster into false independent breadth. It is added to
the St. Mary's record as corroborating context, not as a new Candidate.

Independent of that exhibit, a Paradise Garage oral history includes direct
testimony from dancers Kat Ayala and Willie Dancer about membership, clothing,
floor practice, and the room's dancer-centered design. Head of security Joey
Llanos also describes the crowd's initial rejection of “Heartbeat” and its
immediate retail demand after repeated plays. This strengthens dancer agency
inside two existing Garage-related Candidates without pretending the Bronx and
downtown scenes were one community.

- [Bronx County Historical Society — Rocking, 1971–77](https://bronxhistoricalsociety.org/dance/3)
  supports named dancers, St. Mary's practice, competition, and movement.
- [Bronx County Historical Society — Burning, 1971–75](https://bronxhistoricalsociety.org/dance/4)
  supports Black Bronx dancer agency and the dancer/DJ/record-selection
  relationship.
- [Time Out New York — Paradise Garage oral history](https://www.timeout.com/newyork/nightlife/paradise-garage-the-oral-history-of-nycs-greatest-club)
  supports named dancer testimony and the “Heartbeat” floor/store account.

### 4. Did the New York Record Pool carry feedback to labels?

Resolved at the institutional-mechanism level. Billboard's June 21, 1975
launch report describes a non-profit pool for about 60 working DJs representing
many leading local clubs. Its cover letter promised member product critiques
when participating labels requested them. The same report records a
contemporary concern: collective programming or rejection could produce
political bargaining rather than neutral audience evidence.

Billboard's August 1975 reports add two concrete directions: labels sent test
pressings through the Pool, and Pool activity brought a Tina Charles record to
Columbia's attention. Together these Sources support a two-way DJ/label
information channel. They do not prove that a particular dancer response
caused a particular mix, signing, pressing quantity, or release decision.

- [Billboard, June 21, 1975 — “Disco DJs Form a ‘Pool’”](https://www.worldradiohistory.com/Archive-All-Music/Billboard/70s/1975/Billboard%201975-06-21.pdf)
  supports launch scale, working-DJ membership, label distribution, requested
  critiques, and contemporary concern about collective programming power.
- [Billboard, August 2, 1975](https://www.worldradiohistory.com/Archive-All-Music/Billboard/70s/1975/Billboard%201975-08-02.pdf)
  supports test-pressing distribution through the Pool.
- [Billboard, August 9, 1975](https://www.worldradiohistory.com/Archive-Billboard/70s/1975/Billboard%201975-08-09.pdf)
  supports the reported Tina Charles-to-Columbia example.

### 5. Did Gallery or Leviticus promotion prove club-to-label feedback?

No. The accessible evidence supports label access and promotion, not a closed
feedback mechanism. The Gallery received free records and Nicky Siano watched
his dancers. Leviticus hosted recurring label press parties. Neither Source set
documents floor-response reports returning to a label or changing a release.

These Candidates remain useful because they show different interfaces among
clubs, audiences, and record companies. Stage 05 must not turn those interfaces
into feedback arrows. The Record Pool is the only current Candidate with
contemporary evidence of an organized critique channel.

### 6. Can one later Paradise Garage record/store/radio moment be bounded?

Partly resolved through a new Candidate. In 1981, Larry Levan repeatedly played
his remix of Taana Gardner's “Heartbeat” after the Garage floor initially
rejected it; Joey Llanos recalls customers lining up at Vinyl Mania the next
day. A contemporary April 1981 trade chart confirms the record's release and
club-chart presence. This supports a bounded club-to-retail circulation moment,
not a controlled causal proof of national sales or radio programming.

The broader institutional account says records Levan played appeared at
Vinylmania and on radio, but the accessible evidence does not tie “Heartbeat”
to one documented radio decision. Radio is therefore excluded from the new
Candidate's Event boundary.

- [Time Out New York — Paradise Garage oral history](https://www.timeout.com/newyork/nightlife/paradise-garage-the-oral-history-of-nycs-greatest-club)
  supports the named witness's floor-rejection, repeated-play, and next-day
  store-demand account.
- [Record World, April 11, 1981](https://www.worldradiohistory.com/Archive-Record-World/80s/81/RW-1981-04-11.pdf)
  supports the contemporary release identity and club-chart presence.
- [NYC LGBTQ Historic Sites Project — Paradise Garage](https://www.nyclgbtsites.org/site/paradise-garage/)
  supports the broader Garage, Vinylmania, and radio pattern without proving a
  record-specific radio decision.

### 7. What challenges a neat A+C integration?

The evidence challenges it in three ways:

1. Best of Friends has a documented multi-borough ownership network, but no
   Source found in this stage connects its Queens, Brooklyn, or Leviticus
   operations to New York Record Pool membership or feedback records.
2. Bronx Hustle, Rocking, and Burning circulated through recreation centers,
   streets, basements, community rooms, competitions, and later clubs. Their
   dancer-led development is not reducible to label promotion or a downtown
   record-distribution organization.
3. Billboard's launch report warned that pooled DJ feedback could become
   collective programming leverage. The feedback system therefore had power,
   incentives, and conflict; it was not a transparent measurement of “the
   dance floor.”

The revised thesis must describe intersecting and parallel infrastructures,
not one integrated citywide machine.

hypothesis is:

> New York underground Disco grew through several forms of collective power

> organized channels linking records, floor response, labels, and specialist
