## Synthesis method and evidence boundary

This stage compares the seven fixed Event dossiers without adding historical
research. A dossier claim ID below points back to the claim-specific Source and
confidence recorded in Stage 06. Relationships are marked `documented` only
when the dossiers identify a direct shared actor, place, adaptation, or
institutional continuity. Everything else is labeled
`interpretive-synthesis`, even when the comparison is historically useful.

The route does not describe one seamless Disco network. It tests how different
groups organized a dance floor, a room, a business, a distribution channel,
or a record's circulation—and where those forms of collective power met,
remained parallel, or produced unequal authority.

## Direct connections, synthesis, and convergence

Direct documented connections are limited to three relationship records:

1. `rel-loft-gallery-adaptation` — Siano's named Loft attendance and adaptation;
2. `rel-loft-pool-organizer-place-change` — Mancuso across two distinct places
   and institutional forms; and
3. `rel-gallery-garage-work-continuity` plus
   `rel-garage-heartbeat-place-practice` — named personnel or place continuity
   across separately bounded Events.

The remaining records are explicit comparisons. They do not establish contact
or influence. The route's convergence is therefore thematic and structural:
by 1975, rooms, dancers, owners, DJs, and labels were participating in several
different systems for selecting, presenting, discussing, and circulating
records. Those systems sometimes shared people or places, but authority stayed
uneven and no single system represented “the dance floor.”

Parallel development remains visible in the overlap among St. Mary's,
Leviticus, the Gallery, and the Pool. Their shared years cannot be converted
into a sequence in which Bronx practice feeds downtown clubs, label parties
produce Pool feedback, or the Gallery automatically produces the Garage.

## Final working thesis

> New York underground Disco took shape when people organized collective
> listening and dancing into different kinds of power. Dancers developed
> practices in community and club spaces; Black entrepreneurs built nightlife
> institutions; hosts, DJs, designers, and workers shaped rooms around music;
> and DJs organized channels between promotional records and labels. These
> systems sometimes shared people and places, but they were never one seamless
> network. Following their intersections and limits reveals how a floor could
> become a social institution, a source of information, and—without becoming
> an automatic judge of success—an active participant in a record's life.

This thesis preserves Arc A plus Arc C as the primary institutional mechanism
and uses Arc B as a continuing corrective: the story must name dancers and
communities as historical actors while acknowledging that the surviving
Sources more often preserve owners, DJs, organizers, and male narrators.

## Structure assessment

### Event-centric option

A seven-stop chronological sequence would make every Event immediately
scannable and reduce grouping complexity. It would also misrepresent the dense
1974–77 overlap as a causal chain, repeat “another club” framing across Loft,
Leviticus, Gallery, and Garage, and leave the route thesis to short transitions
rather than showing how different mechanisms compare.

### Argument-centric option — selected

Four movements group Events by historical work: making collective floors,
turning rooms into institutions and interfaces, organizing record exchange,
and testing a purpose-built floor through one record. This structure keeps
every Event a self-contained chapter while making the contrasts do visible
work. It is selected because chronology alone cannot explain the simultaneous
developments, and because the single-Event Pool section needs structural
weight rather than being absorbed into club promotion.

The selected structure does not require a fixed reading order in the product.
Each Event blueprint re-establishes its people, action, place, time, and route
function so the chapter remains legible when opened independently.

## Narrative-section records

### `section-making-collective-floors`

- Title: Making collective floors
- Historical question: How did people organize recurring spaces where dancing
  and listening became practices rather than incidental entertainment?
- Narrative function: establish two distinct forms of collective practice
  outside a conventional commercial-club account.
- Ordered Event IDs:
  1. `loft-broadway-party-1970-1974`
  2. `st-marys-dance-incubator-1974`
- Grouping rationale: both Events center recurring gatherings and dancer
  practice, but their hosts, communities, places, music histories, and
  authority differ. The comparison opens the route without proposing contact.
- Order basis: the Loft has the earlier bounded start; St. Mary's then shifts
  agency and geography to Puerto Rican teenagers in a Bronx community center.
- Accidental-causality check: pass only if the transition explicitly says the
  developments are parallel and does not imply the Loft taught or enabled
  St. Mary's.
- Transition into section: begin with the practical work of assembling a
  recurring dance floor, not with a universal origin claim.
- Transition out: widen from collectively made practices to the different
  ownership, commercial, promotional, and room-design choices that shaped
  mid-1970s institutions.
- Presentation implication: compare a Broadway residence and Bronx recreation
  center as distinct place types; do not draw a route of influence.
- Unresolved claim gaps: St. Mary's end date, coordinate provenance, fuller
  testimony from women and ordinary dancers, and the Loft's broad influence
  remain unresolved.

### `section-building-rooms-and-interfaces`

- Title: Building rooms and interfaces
- Historical question: Who controlled nightlife institutions, and how did
  ownership, room design, dancers, and label access meet without becoming the
  same kind of power?
- Narrative function: place Black ownership at the center through Leviticus,
  then examine the Gallery's different room-level coordination of crowd,
  sound, lighting, labor, and supplied records.
- Ordered Event IDs:
  1. `leviticus-opening-and-label-parties-1974-1976`
  2. `gallery-mercer-room-1974-1977`
- Grouping rationale: both venues provide documented label interfaces and
  institutional choices, but Leviticus's route work is Black ownership and
  promotion while the Gallery's is commercial room adaptation and dancer/DJ/
  design interaction.
- Order basis: conceptual contrast, not chronology; Leviticus first protects
  ownership from being reduced to audience composition before the Gallery
  chapter examines a predominantly Black gay floor whose ownership differs.
- Accidental-causality check: pass only if no link between the venues is
  implied and label parties/free records remain distinct from feedback.
- Transition into section: move from practices made in recurring gatherings
  to the authority embedded in owning, financing, designing, staffing, and
  programming rooms.
- Transition out: ask what changes when record exchange is organized outside a
  single venue and critique becomes an explicit institutional offer.
- Presentation implication: Midtown and Mercer Street are comparison nodes;
  proximity to other downtown places is not influence.
- Unresolved claim gaps: exact Leviticus opening day, Gallery dancer testimony,
  label-specific outcomes, coordinate provenance, and item rights remain open.

### `section-organizing-record-exchange`

- Title: Organizing record exchange
- Historical question: What did working DJs gain—and what representative
  authority did they not gain—when promotional distribution and critique were
  organized collectively?
- Narrative function: give the non-venue Pool mechanism enough space to
  explain distribution, requested critiques, organizational leverage, and
  limits without conflating it with club promotion.
- Ordered Event IDs:
  1. `new-york-record-pool-launch-1975`
- Grouping rationale: the Pool is the sole accepted Event with contemporary
  evidence of this formal channel; grouping it with a club would weaken the
  distinction the route needs to make.
- Order basis: thematic pivot inside the overlapping 1975 chronology.
- Accidental-causality check: pass only if shared actors or nearby places do not
  imply that Loft, Leviticus, or Gallery dancers became Pool members or that
  critiques directly changed label decisions.
- Transition into section: distinguish publicity and access from a written
  offer to collect product critiques.
- Transition out: return from organized information infrastructure to a later
  room built around dancers, then test how uncertain and resistant a real floor
  response could be.
- Presentation implication: show 99 Prince Street as an infrastructure point,
  separate from the Broadway Loft; organizational documents are leads, not
  approved media.
- Unresolved claim gaps: exact founding roles, original forms and minutes,
  record-level outcomes, representativeness, coordinate provenance, and rights.

### `section-building-and-testing-the-garage-floor`

- Title: Building and testing the Garage floor
- Historical question: What becomes possible when a club is deliberately
  built around music and dancing, and what does one later record reveal about
  the limits of DJ and floor authority?
- Narrative function: establish the Garage as a constructed membership
  institution, then use “Heartbeat” as a bounded, attributed test of audience
  resistance, repetition, and reported specialist-retail demand.
- Ordered Event IDs:
  1. `paradise-garage-buildout-opening-1977-1978`
  2. `heartbeat-garage-vinylmania-1981`
- Grouping rationale: the same place and Larry Levan connect the Events across
  time, but their boundaries and functions differ: institution-building first,
  record circulation later.
- Order basis: documented chronology and necessary place context.
- Accidental-causality check: pass only if the opening is not said to cause the
  record's release or success and the witness account remains visibly
  retrospective.
- Transition into section: contrast a formal distribution organization with a
  purpose-built room whose members could still reject a DJ's selection.
- Transition out: close on collective response as consequential but partial,
  contested, and insufficient by itself to explain a record's wider life.
- Presentation implication: the Garage point changes function across time; a
  later original Vinylmania point or movement needs provenance and qualified
  wording.
- Unresolved claim gaps: construction-party start, ordinary-member voices,
  exact 1981 night, contemporary corroboration of the next-day line, store
  geometry, playable media, and rights.

## Event narrative blueprints

### `loft-broadway-party-1970-1974`

- Assigned section ID: `section-making-collective-floors`
- Reader question: What did Mancuso and invited dancers build when a home,
  sound system, full records, hospitality, and recurring membership became one
  party practice?
- Opening move: lead with the documented February 14, 1970 start inside the
  Broadway residence and identify Mancuso as host rather than inventing a
  dance-floor scene.
- Ordered narrative beats:
  1. Bound the invitation-only weekly house party at 645–647 Broadway
     (`loft-01`, `loft-02`).
  2. Show how dancing, refreshments, no alcohol, careful sound, and full-record
     presentation formed an integrated practice (`loft-02`–`loft-04`).
  3. Name the mixed LGBT and straight membership without turning inclusive
     attendance into collective ownership (`loft-03`).
  4. End with Siano's documented attendance and later adaptation as one named
     connection, not a universal origin story (`loft-05`,
     `rel-loft-gallery-adaptation`).
- Reader-visible uncertainty or qualification: describe the Loft as one
  influential private-party model, never the single birthplace or template for
  every later club; keep Broadway separate from Prince Street.
- Reader-copy structure recommendation: one continuous passage with two clear
  paragraphs—first the gathering and its practice, then its bounded influence.
  A subsection heading would add hierarchy without a genuine change of idea.
- Transition role and route handoff: open the route at the scale of a hosted
  room, then hand to St. Mary's as a parallel community-space history in which
  teenagers, rather than a single host, drive the practice.

### `st-marys-dance-incubator-1974`

- Assigned section ID: `section-making-collective-floors`
- Reader question: How did Puerto Rican teenagers use recurring St. Mary's
  gatherings to develop and carry Latin Hustle and Rocking practices?
- Opening move: begin at the documented summer-1974 recreation-center parties
  and name the teenage dancers and organizers; do not begin with a generalized
  borough or origin claim.
- Ordered narrative beats:
  1. Establish the recurring St. Mary's parties and safer-gathering intention
     with attributed organizer evidence (`stmarys-01`, `stmarys-02`).
  2. Put Latin Hustle and Rocking side by side through named dancers, practice,
     and competition rather than flattening them into one form (`stmarys-03`).
  3. Use the documented images and accounts to keep girls and dancer groups
     visible without inventing atmosphere (`stmarys-04`).
  4. Describe later movement toward competitions and Manhattan clubs only as a
     qualified path whose exact dates and destinations are incomplete
     (`stmarys-05`).
- Reader-visible uncertainty or qualification: the Event's end date is
  unknown; St. Mary's is an incubator within a wider dance history, not the sole
  birthplace of a dance or genre; violence claims require attribution.
- Reader-copy structure recommendation: two subsections are warranted—one for
  the gatherings and practices, one for how dancers carried them outward—so
  the uncertain movement history does not blur the well-supported Event core.
- Transition role and route handoff: complete the opening comparison by naming
  dancer/community agency, then widen to the different powers exercised by
  owners, staff, DJs, designers, and labels in formal nightlife rooms.
